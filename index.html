About

Physics as a lens on computational methods

I'm a computational researcher. My PhD in Electrical and Computer Engineering at Purdue, with Sabre Kais and Zubin Jacob, sat between developing numerical methods for quantum information, electronic structure and photonics. During my Ph.D., I also developed the ML side of HADAR, a thermal-imaging method that was published as a Nature cover article.

At JPMorgan Chase's Global Technology Applied Research center, I work on quantum and classical numerical methods at scale, from quantum optimization to optimal transport solvers for exotic derivative pricing, and methods that has to survive production.

What I work on

Computational materials and physics-informed ML. When a DFT label can be trusted, what symmetry fixes in a transport calculation, and ML models that carry the physics rather than fit around it (HADAR, Nature 2023).

Algorithms for hard optimization. Parameter setting and trainability for QAOA (Quantum 2024, Nature Communications 2024), and solvers for large martingale optimal transport problems.

Quantum information and learning. Variational methods for physical systems.

The thread through all of it is the same question, wherein I'd like to understand what structure does a problem have, which quantities can a method be trusted to get right, and where will it fail without telling you? That question gets sharper in AI for science, where the data a model learns from is itself the output of an approximate calculation.

Computational Materials and Physics-Informed ML

Machine learning for materials is only as good as the calculations that label it. This work asks where those labels can be trusted, uses symmetry to separate real effects from artefacts of a model, and builds ML that carries physical structure instead of fitting around it.

Label fragility in DFT magnetic moments

Stratified about 26,000 Materials Project structures by theory-chosen features, held out whole physics regions, and ran DFT+U scans in Quantum ESPRESSO to measure how far recorded moments move with U. The failure regions of a finetuned M3GNet and a zero-shot CHGNet line up with ions whose ligand field competes with Hund's coupling at their actual charge state. Then opened CHGNet up with linear probes and activation ablations to ask whether it represents its own error, and which of those representations the network actually computes rather than reads off the chemical formula.

Physics-stratified training data for magnetic property prediction Report, 2026

Symmetry-controlled readout in altermagnetic junctions

NEGF transport through a resonant level coupled to two d-wave altermagnetic electrodes. Texture orientation sets the sign of the magnetic readout, and a reflection symmetry forces it to vanish at 45°, checked against a matched ferromagnetic control.

Personal independent work

Independent studies in computational condensed matter

Improving training data for magnetic property prediction

Materials Project magnetic moments come from DFT+U, with the Hubbard U fitted to reproduce formation energies, not moments. Near a spin-state crossover the moment turns on that choice of U. A different but equally defensible value flips the ion between a high-spin and a low-spin solution, and the database records one of them with no sign that the other exists. I wanted to ask whether models trained on those labels inherit the ambiguity, and whether theory can flag the fragile labels before a single calculation is run. My first hypothesis, d-electron count, was wrong. The one that held is about the ligand field rather than the count. What matters is whether, at an ion's actual charge state, it competes with Hund's coupling. Co³⁺ reaches that regime, Co²⁺ does not, despite sharing d⁷ with Ni³⁺.

Then, trying to keep up with the state of the art interpretability research in large networks, I decided to open the model up to understand whether CHGNet already represents what it gets wrong. It does, though the circuit I first found for it, dissolved under a control I should have run at the start.

Orientation-dependent readout in a model altermagnetic junction

Can the orientation of a momentum-dependent spin splitting decide how a junction reads out its magnetic state? In a minimal NEGF model of a d-wave altermagnetic junction, it does. Rotate one electrode's spin texture by 90° and the readout flips sign. Turn it to 45° and the two magnetic states become electrically indistinguishable. That isn't a tuned coincidence. It follows from a reflection symmetry of the transport channels, which the calculation reproduces to floating-point precision.

I got there because an earlier gate-tuned "sweet spot" also showed up in the ferromagnetic control, so it couldn't be telling me anything about altermagnets.
